Film came back, and Bolexes came back with it. A new generation has picked these cameras up and started shooting with them again, which is the best thing that has happened to them in forty years. It also means they cost more than they did, and that the question we get asked most often is some version of *what is mine worth*.

The honest answer is a method rather than a number, because a Bolex sells for whatever a buyer is willing to pay for it. Here is how to find out what that is.

## Read sold listings, not asking prices

This is the one habit worth building, and it is where most people go wrong.

eBay is full of Bolex gear sitting at optimistic prices, listed and relisted for months without selling. Nothing is wrong with trying your luck, but those numbers are hopes rather than sales, and if you average them you will conclude your camera is worth two or three times what it is.

The filter you want is free and slightly buried:

1. Search for the model, then open **Filter**, or scroll the left-hand sidebar.
2. Tick **Sold items**. Completed items includes the ones that never sold, which is the thing you are trying to avoid.
3. Sort by most recent and skip anything that is obviously a different model, a mixed lot, or a parts camera.

That gives roughly the last ninety days of real transactions, and the prices are usually well below what is sitting unsold. The trade-off is patience: a seller priced at the actual market is rarer than one priced at the dream, so you may be waiting a while for the right listing. Worth it.

If you sell on eBay yourself, Seller Hub has a research tool reaching back about three years instead of ninety days, free, under **Research → Product research**.

## Auctions land close to fair

An auction price is what two people who both wanted the thing were prepared to pay on the day, which is about as close to a true market value as this equipment gets. They have become a genuinely good way to buy and sell at a sensible number. Camera specialists publish their past results, and an eBay listing that ran as a real auction rather than a fixed price is the same idea in miniature.

## Marketplace is busy, and depends entirely on where you are

Facebook Marketplace has been getting livelier every year and is now a real hot spot for Bolexes. The catch is that it is intensely local. The same camera can be cheap in one city and dear in another, depending on who happens to be clearing a cupboard near you that week. Brilliant for a patient local buyer, close to useless as a guide to what anything is worth.

## The user groups keep everyone honest

Which is where the groups come in, and for a lot of this gear they are the best place of all. The Bolex group on Facebook and the cinematography communities are full of people who have been doing this for years, and buying and selling happens there constantly.

The difference is the feedback. Post something at a silly number and somebody will gently say so; post something underpriced and somebody will tell you that too. Between them, buyers and sellers get nudged towards what things are actually trading for this month. It is a market with its own referees, which is more than a search results page can offer. ## Reading the number once you have it

**Condition is most of the price.** A recently serviced body and an untested one are the same model and nowhere near the same money. This was true when they were new, too: Paillard's own service centres charged $48.00 to overhaul an H camera when the camera itself was around $300.00. A service still costs a real fraction of what the camera is worth, and that comes off the price.

**The glass is often worth more than the body.** A turret with three good Switars is a different object from the same camera bare, and one rare lens can carry an otherwise ordinary outfit.

**Three sales is not a market.** This gear changes hands rarely enough that any figure you reach is a range. Take the middle of several recent sold results and treat the extremes as noise.

## The deals are still out there

The days of a reflex Bolex for $300 to $500 are behind us, and it is no use pretending otherwise. But patient people still find good ones at fair money, every week, usually by knowing what fair money is before they start looking. That is the whole trick.
